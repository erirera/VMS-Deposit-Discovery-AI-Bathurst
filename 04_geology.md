# Regional Geological Setting and Mineralization

## Tectonic Setting of the Bathurst Mining Camp

The Bathurst Mining Camp (BMC) in northern New Brunswick forms part of the Gander Zone in the northern Appalachian Orogen (Van Staal et al., 2003). The camp records the development and accretionary deformation of the Cambro-Ordovician Tetagouche-Four Falls back-arc basin, which opened via rifting of the Popelogan volcanic arc along the Gander passive margin of Gondwana. The closing of this back-arc basin during the Late Ordovician to Silurian accretionary phases (Salinic and Acadian orogenies) resulted in intense crustal shortening, generating a complex, structurally repeated collage of thrust sheets (nappes) that now comprise the BMC (Goodfellow and McCutcheon, 2003).

## Stratigraphy and Host Formations

The stratigraphy of the BMC is divided into several volcanic and sedimentary groups thrust over the quartzose meta-sedimentary rocks of the cambro-Ordovician Miramichi Group, which forms the depositional basement of the camp:

1.  **Miramichi Group:** Comprises quartz-rich sandstones, quartzites, and dark shales representing passive margin sediment accumulation prior to rifting.
2.  **Tetagouche Group:** Host to the majority of VMS deposits in the camp (including the giant Brunswick No. 12 and Brunswick No. 6 deposits). It is subdivided into:
    *   *Nepisiguit Falls Formation:* Composed of quartz-feldspar crystal-rich volcanic sands (tuffs), fine-grained volcaniclastic sediments, and minor rhyolite flows. Most major VMS deposits are spatially associated with the contact between these felsic volcanic sediments and overlying fine sedimentary rocks.
    *   *Flat Landing Brook Formation:* Dominated by thick rhyolite flows, dome complexes, and co-genetic felsic hyaloclastites.
    *   *Boucher Brook Formation:* Predominantly basic volcanic rocks (tholeiitic pillow basalts) and sedimentary rocks (black shales, cherts, and iron formations).
3.  **California Lake Group:** Host to several VMS deposits (e.g., Caribou and Restigouche). It contains a similar sequence of felsic volcanic units (e.g., Spruce Lake Formation) and basic volcanic units (Mount Brittain Formation).
4.  **Four Falls Group:** Composed of a distinct tholeiitic basaltic volcanism sequence representing more mature back-arc oceanic crust.

## VMS Deposit Characteristics and Mineralization

The VMS deposits in the BMC are classified as the **bimodal-siliciclastic subtype** (Galley et al., 2007). These deposits typically consist of stratiform, lens-shaped, or sheet-like massive sulfide bodies associated with hydrothermal discharge vents. The massive sulfide lenses generally exhibit a distinct mineral and geochemical zonation from footwall to hanging wall:
*   **Footwall Stringer (Stockwork) Zone:** Characterized by network veins of chalcopyrite, pyrrhotite, and pyrite hosted within highly silicified and chloritized felsic volcanic rocks.
*   **Bedded Massive Sulfide Lens:** Composed of fine-grained, banded pyrite, sphalerite, and galena, hosting major zinc, lead, silver, and gold mineralization.
*   **Hanging Wall Iron Formation:** Covered by jasper-bearing or magnetitic chert horizons, representing late-stage oxidized hydrothermal sedimentation.

Hydrothermal alteration is zoned around the feeder pipes, progressing from an intense quartz-iron-chlorite core in the footwall stringer zone outward to sericite-quartz-carbonate-pyrite halos (Goodfellow, 2007).

## Structural Geology and Deformation

The BMC has undergone progressive, polyphase deformation ($D_1$ to $D_4$) under greenschist-facies metamorphism (Van Staal et al., 2003):
*   **$D_1$ Deformation:** Characterized by early thrust-related folding and sub-horizontal ductile shear zones, which stacked the back-arc sequences into thrust nappes.
*   **$D_2$ Deformation:** Produced tight to isoclinal folding with a strong penetrative schistosity, structurally repeating the thin sulfide horizons.
*   **$D_3$ and $D_4$ Deformation:** Generated large-scale open folds, dome-and-basin interference geometries (e.g., the Brunswick anticline), and late brittle fault systems.

This intense deformation has dismembered, folded, and structurally thickened the originally planar massive sulfide deposits, complicating deposit targeting and making structural geophysics a critical exploratory input.
