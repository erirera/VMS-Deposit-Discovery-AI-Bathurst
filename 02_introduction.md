# Introduction

Volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) deposits are major global repositories of base metals (copper, zinc, lead) and associated precious metals (gold, silver). Several commodities associated with VMS deposits—including zinc, copper, indium, bismuth, tin, and antimony—are classified as critical minerals necessary for green energy transition technologies (Franklin et al., 2005; Galley et al., 2007). Historically, VMS discovery relied on identifying shallow, outcropping mineralization. However, near-surface deposits in mature exploration districts have been largely exhausted, requiring exploration programs to target deeply buried or covered systems beneath glacial overburden (Goodfellow and McCutcheon, 2003). In glaciated terrains, targeting covered systems requires the quantitative integration of regional multi-physics geophysical surveys and till geochemistry.

The Cambro-Ordovician Bathurst Mining Camp (BMC) in northern New Brunswick, Canada, is one of the most prolific VMS districts globally, historically hosting over 45 known deposits (Goodfellow, 2007). The camp is characterized by structurally complex, bimodal volcanic-sedimentary sequences of the Tetagouche Group, which have undergone intense polyphase deformation and ductile shear zone development (Van Staal et al., 2003). This high degree of deformation, combined with variable thicknesses of glacial till, masks the direct surface expressions of mineralized zones. Consequently, machine learning (ML)-based mineral prospectivity mapping (MPM) has emerged as a powerful framework to integrate camp-scale datasets and delineate prospective target areas (Harris et al., 2015; Carranza, 2017).

To construct a physically robust prospectivity model, preprocessing must respect the mathematical and physical nature of the geoscientific input data. Geophysical methods, such as high-resolution aeromagnetic and gravity surveys, map structural shear zones and mass anomalies. In traditional workflows, geophysical derivatives (e.g., First Vertical Derivative, Total Horizontal Gradient, and Tilt Derivative) are often computed on reprojected, interpolated regional grids. However, this introduces edge effects, grid boundary artifacts, and distorts high-frequency structural gradients. Computing these derivatives in the Fourier domain directly on the original survey grids prior to spatial resampling preserves high-frequency boundary information and represents a more mathematically consistent approach to structural mapping (Thomas et al., 2000). Radiometric data further constrains VMS targeting by mapping radioelement ratios (e.g., K/Th, U/Th), which respond to potassic and sericitic alteration zones caused by hydrothermal vent systems (Shives et al., 1997).

Geochemical datasets present distinct mathematical challenges due to their compositional nature. Geochemical concentrations are closed data (summing to $10^6$ ppm or 100%), which imposes a constant-sum constraint that introduces spurious correlations and invalidates classical multivariate statistics, such as standard Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Factor Analysis (FA) (Aitchison, 1986). To resolve this, compositional data analysis (CoDA) methods, specifically the Centered Log-Ratio (CLR) transformation, are required to project compositional variables from the simplex into real space (Egozcue et al., 2003). While CLR-based PCA and FA score rasters (interpolated via IDW) capture regional multi-element hydrothermal trends, regional interpolation inevitably oversmooths local, high-contrast geochemical anomalies. Integrating *raw* geochemical concentration values at point locations alongside interpolated surfaces ensures that the high-contrast, local pathfinder signatures are not diluted by spatial smoothing (Parkhill and Doiron, 2003).

Finally, machine learning models applied to MPM must address spatial autocorrelation and spatial data leakage. Standard random k-fold cross-validation violates the independent and identically distributed (i.e.d.) assumption due to spatial clustering, leading to overly optimistic model validation metrics (Roberts et al., 2017). Implementing a spatial block cross-validation framework restricts training and validation folds to distinct geographic blocks, providing a realistic estimate of the model's generalization capability. Furthermore, evaluating these models solely on Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and Precision-Recall (PR) curves is insufficient for exploration planning. While ROC-AUC measures overall class separation, it does not assess targeting efficiency relative to the physical area. Cumulative Success Rate (or Prediction-Area) curves plot the percentage of discovered deposits against the percentage of prospective area occupied, providing a direct metric of exploration efficiency that is critical for managing drilling economics (Carranza, 2008).

This study addresses these challenges by developing a camp-scale VMS prospectivity mapping pipeline in the Bathurst Mining Camp. We compute vertical and horizontal geophysical derivatives on the original survey grids, compile a unified 17-element till geochemistry dataset using compositional transformations, and integrate raw geochemical pathfinder footprints alongside multi-element anomaly scores (MEAS). Using spatial block cross-validation and class balancing (SMOTE), we train and evaluate Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers. We assess model performance using ROC, PR, and cumulative Success Rate curves, demonstrating that the inclusion of raw geochemistry features—particularly lead (`pb_ppm`) and iron (`fe_ppm`)—dramatically improves targeting efficiency and provides a geophysically and geochemically consistent framework for covered VMS exploration.
